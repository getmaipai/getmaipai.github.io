Design decisions

The full design history (2,900-line plan, layout, wiring schedule, assembly runbook, three audits) lives in the private pre-migration repository. This page keeps the decisions that shape the code and the build, with the why.

Standalone first

The robot works with no internet, no hub, and no router. Nothing is fetched at run time; models are baked into the image and checked at boot. Hub features are optional and injected, and every one has a spoken “I can’t do that offline” fallback. This is the rule most easily broken by accident (most voice and vision libraries lazy-download by default), so it is enforced by tests, not manners.

The face

Two portrait ST7789 displays draw amber pill-shaped eyes on black behind a smoked visor, with the camera centred between them as the nose. The eyes carry emotion only, never status (no battery bars in an eye). The mouth is a 16x8 LED matrix that moves with the robot’s own speech and expression: abstract dots, never drawn lips, never a music visualiser. Centre columns lit by default because the panel is wider than the eye span. The expression set (neutral, happy, wink, curious, surprised, scared, sleepy, angry, content) keeps an 80/20 positive-to-negative ratio, and a sharp pose is never shown while the gaze is on a person.

Three surfaces

Glance → the heart knob. Interact → the touchscreen. Across the room → the underglow. State may appear on all three (privacy state must); input never duplicates: configuration changes only on the touchscreen, the knob only mutes and rotates, the underglow takes no input. Voice leads all of it.

Privacy is announced, not physical

There is no lens cover and no microphone cut switch. A hardware switch is only honest if it interrupts something, and on a CSI camera there is nothing to interrupt: power arrives over the ribbon mixed in with the MIPI pairs and I2C, so a switch there would be a software signal with a light on it. That reads like a guarantee and is not one. Rather than ship one honest switch (the USB mic’s 5 V really can be cut) and one theatrical one, both went, and the mic and camera are controlled by voice and the heart knob.

What carries the promise instead: nothing leaves the house, recording is adult-only and explicitly asked for, and it is never covert - the recording heartbeat drives an indicator on all three surfaces at once. The robot can prove when it is capturing. It cannot prove that it is not, and nothing in these docs may imply otherwise. A household that wants a guarantee software cannot break covers the lens or unplugs the array.

The neck reports its own angle

A hobby servo is open loop: it is told an angle and never says whether it got there. A stall, a slipped horn, a stripped gear and a clean move all look the same from the Pi. An AS5600 magnetic encoder on the joint reads where the head actually is, which is the rule above applied to the neck: physical state is measured, never assumed.

It earns its place twice. A head-mounted mic array needs the pan angle at capture time before a bearing means anything, and the IMU cannot supply it: the MPU-6050 has no magnetometer, so integrated gyro yaw drifts.

The mic ring says one thing: you are in a conversation

The microphone array carries a 12-LED ring. It is not a privacy indicator, and treating it as one would be a mistake we can point at evidence for.

Ahmad et al. (CSCW 2022, N=261) crossed physical against software mute control with physical, LED and software feedback. Hardware control improved trust and perceived control decisively; the feedback mechanism moved neither (both hypotheses rejected). Portnoff et al. (CHI 2015) found 45% of people notice a webcam LED 50 cm from their face, and 5% while concentrating on something else. In two studies of what people actually worry about with smart speakers, the light ring is not mentioned once. A light across the room, on a device sold as eyes-free, is not what earns trust. The switch that cuts the 5 V is, and that is already the rule above.

So the ring gets one meaning, which it is good at: the robot is in a conversation with you.

StateRing
Idle, listening for its nameoff
Listening to you (including multi-turn)slow amber pulse, the brighter of the two
Heard you, thinkingsolid, dimmed, same colour
Speakingoff: the eyes and mouth are its speaking presence
Recording (adult-initiated)solid red
Muteddark, because its 5 V is cut

The states differ by motion, not only by colour, so they survive colour blindness and a glance: a slow pulse against a steady glow, the pulse brighter of the two.

Brightness follows the room, not the clock. The array faces upward and lights the room rather than sitting on a shelf, and the usable band measured far lower and narrower than the hardware’s range suggests: full brightness was painful in the dark, and a pulse dim enough to be polite was invisible, because a pulse spends half its cycle fading toward nothing. A measured ambient level decides which column is used; the clock answers only when nothing can see, since someone asleep at two in the afternoon behind drawn shades and a bright kitchen at midnight both defeat it. Every number is a default a household can override. Amazon’s own worst case is instructive: Error is a quickly pulsing purple and Do Not Disturb a slowly pulsing purple, two unrelated states separated by nothing but rate.

Nothing lights during passive listening. No major assistant does it, and a light that is always on carries no information. The board ships in LED_EFFECT 4 (direction mode), which reacts to any sound in the room; that gets turned off.

No direction of arrival on the ring. We cannot: on the USB board the firmware owns the ring and the host’s direction argument is discarded, and 12 LEDs quantise to 30 degrees. We also should not. Amazon retired directional cyan on the 2025 Echo Dot Max and Echo Studio in favour of a fixed pair of pulses whose width follows your voice level, trading “where you are” for “how loud you are”. No study shows a directional light improves trust, and a wedge aimed at the wrong person is worse than no wedge. The direction is for the neck: a robot that turns to look at you says it better than any light.

The light has a priority order, and it pre-empts rather than blends

States are not enough on their own: something has to say what happens when two are true at once. Recording while thinking. An error during a sentence. Without an order, that decision gets made accidentally at whichever call site fires last.

Highest first: recording, listening, thinking, speaking, idle. Recording wins because it is the one state shown on every surface at once and must not be hidden by the conversation around it. Listening beats thinking and speaking because it is the only state that wants something from the person. The winner takes the surface completely and the others resume when it ends.

Nothing is ever blended. Two meanings mixed into one colour is neither.

This is the rule the car makers converged on for exactly this problem. Mercedes states it for their light band: system prompts from the driver assistance systems “naturally have a higher priority within the Active Ambient Lighting than the sound visualization function, and therefore temporarily overlay it”. Volkswagen ships a published hierarchy for the ID. Light strip and extends the vocabulary, in their words, “carefully”: six signals across 54 LEDs.

Callers publish what is true and the driver resolves it, so no call site has to know the order and a new state cannot quietly outrank an old one.

On the dock it sleeps, and it wakes the way a person does

A robot that sits on its charger wide awake, staring, is unsettling in a way nobody asks for and everybody feels. So charging is sleeping: eyes closed, ring dark, underglow down to its dark value, no idle behaviour.

Sleep is a posture, not a power state, and the difference matters more than anything else here.

Sleeping never means the microphone is off. A sleeping robot is still listening for its name, exactly as a sleeping person still hears theirs. If “asleep” implied “not listening”, the metaphor would be a lie about privacy, and the one thing that genuinely stops the microphone is the switch that cuts its 5 V. The user page says this in those words.

Three things wake it, and they are the three that wake a person.

WakeResponse
Its nameOpens slowly, looks toward the speaker. The gentle one.
A sudden loud soundStartles: opens fast and wide, looks toward the sound, then settles.
Touch, or being movedOpens, looks at whoever is touching it.

The grading is the point. A person nudged awake and a person woken by a bang do not behave the same, and a robot that responds identically to both reads as a machine running a routine. The startle also settles on its own within a second or two if nothing follows, because that is what a startle is.

Presence alone does not wake it. Someone walking past a sleeping person does not wake them, and a robot that sits up every time anyone crosses the kitchen at three in the morning is a robot that gets unplugged. The presence sensor is for what the robot does while awake.

It goes back to sleep on its own, after a short wait with nothing addressed to it, the way someone dozing off does rather than by snapping shut.

Waking in a dark room is quieter. Dimmer, per the shared brightness rule above, and it does not speak first. Something waking up and immediately talking at you in a dark room is a fright, not a greeting.

Two rules that keep this honest rather than cute. Sleep is never used to look unavailable while the robot is in fact doing something: if it is awake and working, it looks awake. And nothing about sleep is allowed to weaken a safety path: a sleeping robot still answers its emergency stop instantly, because that is a wiring fact and not a behaviour.

The robot senses only the room it is in

The presence radar is a 24 GHz FMCW sensor, and that technology passes through drywall and doors well enough that these parts are sold for through-wall detection. A robot in the hallway can pick up someone breathing in a bedroom.

Nothing about that breaks the privacy promise as written: no data leaves the house either way. It breaks something else, which is that a family should be able to tell what the robot perceives. A machine that quietly knows who is behind a closed door is not the machine we said we were building, however local the data stays.

So the reach is deliberately bounded, and bounded in a way an owner can check:

  • Presence is reported for the robot’s own room, not for the house. The sensitivity and range are set so the sensor answers “someone is here with me”, and detections consistent with being through a wall are discarded rather than quietly used. A robot that cannot tell the difference reports nothing rather than guessing.
  • Presence is a fact about a room, never about a person. It says someone is present. It does not attempt to say who, and it is never joined to identity to infer where a particular family member is. Recognising a person is the camera’s job, in the open, and announced whenever it happens.
  • It is not a log. Presence drives what the robot does now (waking, looking up, sentry). It is not accumulated into a record of when rooms were occupied, because that record is a map of a family’s private routine and nobody asked for it.
  • Closed doors are the line. If the sensor’s usable range would extend beyond the room the robot is standing in, it is turned down until it does not. A shorter range that tells the truth beats a longer one that has to be explained.

The user-tier privacy page gains a plain-language row for it: what it senses, that it works in the dark, that it cannot see who you are, and that it is not written down.

One answer to “is the room dark?”, for every lit surface

Six surfaces light up: the eyes, the mouth, the ring, the heart, the underglow and the touchscreen. Every one of them needs to know whether the room is dark, and if each worked it out for itself they would cross the threshold at slightly different moments and the robot would appear to come apart as the sun went down. So darkness is decided once and every surface renders its own brightness from it. Same shape as the rule below: decide once, render many times.

A measured ambient level decides it, not the clock. The clock is a proxy and a poor one: someone asleep at two in the afternoon behind drawn shades gets a light in the face, and a bright kitchen at midnight gets surfaces nobody can see. The clock answers only when nothing can measure the room, which is a real state rather than a failure: the camera that can estimate ambient light may be muted or busy, and the honest answer then is “I cannot see”, not “it must be daytime”.

Every number is a default and every surface is separately overridable. A value measured in one room is a guess in anyone else’s, and a family with a bright kitchen and a dark hallway cannot be served by one number. Overrides live in advanced settings, per surface, on the touchscreen like every other setting.

Brightness is carried as a percentage because the surfaces have nothing in common underneath: an 8-bit LED byte, a backlight, a panel API. Each driver maps the percentage onto its own hardware, and keeps whatever internal limits it already has. The mouth’s power cap is not negotiable by this table.

The ring’s own pair was measured on the bench rather than chosen from a range: full brightness was painful in a dark room and a polite-looking pulse was invisible, because a pulse spends half its cycle fading toward nothing.

One zone owns attention state

Amazon’s interface guidance is blunt about this: do not duplicate voice chrome, and do not supplement it with other attention-state signifiers. With six lit surfaces (two eyes, mouth, ring, heart, underglow) and a touchscreen, the failure mode is a Christmas tree where every surface says the same thing slightly differently and none of them is trusted.

  • Ordinary conversation state lives on the ring alone. The underglow, the heart and the touchscreen say nothing about it.
  • Recording is the one exception, and it is already required on all three surfaces above. It is the only state important enough to duplicate.
  • The eyes carry emotion, never status, as above.
  • One state is published once and rendered many times. Conversation state joins the expression the face service already publishes; each surface renders its own slice of it. Two surfaces disagreeing is then a bug with an owner, not a judgement call.

Multi-turn is not a new state: a continued conversation is listening. Nor is barge-in, which nowhere in the industry has its own indication and is simply the transition from speaking to listening.

Do no harm outranks every task

The care rules (robot/care/) sit beside the reflex layer in the motion arbiter and can only slow or stop. Unknown floor objects are treated as fragile living things; fleeing animals are never followed; infants and small creatures get the widest margins.

Safety chain in hardware

An e-stop, front and rear bumper switches, and a heartbeat-held spring-applied brake, all in series and all failing toward “stopped”. The software half (robot/brake/) authorises motion only while both brakes report released, and any silence from the IMU is a hard stop.

Body from big-box parts

A hoverboard drivetrain on a plywood deck; a three-tier plastic nightstand as the tower; every precision hole drilled on a flat, replaceable bench panel rather than the curved shell; no 3D printer anywhere. The whole aperture map is marked before the first hole is cut, and all the cable is pulled during the big assembly.

Parts discipline

A part is real only when it has a justification, a home, a wire, a build step, and an order line. For every part, check voltage (the doc that wires it agrees with the doc that buys it), exact connection type (USB-power is not USB-audio), cable length against the real route, current against the supply, physical fit in three dimensions including protrusions, and address/bus/port conflicts. Each of those rules exists because it caught a real mistake.

The dock is dumb

No microcontroller, no data link. The dock is a series arming chain (weight switches, a reed switch keyed by a magnet the robot carries, a leak sensor that fails open) driving a certified enclosed mains switch. The robot holds all the intelligence: a default-closed charge gate that lets a completely dead robot charge, and an INA228 monitor.

A pause after the wake word must not eat the sentence

Found on the bench 2026-09-01, chasing a robot that heard its name and then nothing, forever. Two mechanisms, both triggered by the natural habit of pausing after “hey maipai”:

The deeper one is the transcriber. Moonshine tiny transcribes a real bench recording of “What is your name?” perfectly on its own, and as nothing at all behind half a second of room-level silence (erratically, behind even a fifth of a second). The recognizer buffered everything from the wake word onward, so a talker who paused before their sentence handed Moonshine the words behind exactly that silent head, and the whole turn transcribed to "". Two defenses now: before the VAD hears speech, the buffer keeps only a 0.3 s pre-roll, so the head stays short whatever the pause was; and a transcript that still comes back empty is retried once from the byte where the VAD first heard speech, since clipping a beat off the first word on a retry beats losing the sentence.

The shallower one is the false start. The wake word can fire just before the phrase ends, the leftover syllable trips the VAD, and the pause then “finishes” a turn whose buffer holds a syllable and silence. That empty transcript used to put the robot back to sleep, so the real sentence landed in an empty room, the person said the name again, and the loop repeated (reproduced end to end with no human: the array’s own speaker playing “hey maipai”, a pause, then a sentence, against the real bench.voice chat). The rule that fixes it: an empty transcript inside the wake-patience window is a false start, not a turn. The recognizer reports it (no_speech, “still listening”) and keeps listening; the elapsed clock keeps running, so wake patience still bounds the window and a room whose noise keeps transcribing to nothing releases the robot at the same limit as one where nobody spoke. An empty transcript past that limit closes the window, exactly as before.

The bench states which turn-taking it is proving (fixed timeout or the Smart Turn endpoint) and runs the same optional endpoint the robot does, so a bench pass vouches for the shipped pipeline, not a simpler one.

Conversations become memory

Landed 2026-09-01 (the conversation and memory review in plans/). The robot’s memory is layered like a person’s: what was just said, what the conversation was about, and what is worth keeping.

When a conversation ends (the follow-up window expires, or a goodbye), the turn buffer is summarized into an episode: participants, a two or three sentence summary, the entities that mattered. The local model writes it with grammar-constrained output; offline, an extractive fallback (first ask, last answer, marked as such) keeps the record honest rather than empty. Episodes are episodic-tier and retention-swept like everything else.

The judge runs only while the robot is docked and idle, over episodes and the utterance log since its cursor, and the cursor advances only on success (the hub’s lost-facts lesson). Every fact it extracts carries source_text, the verbatim quote that justifies it, and facts come only from what a person said, never from the robot’s own replies. It updates by accumulate-and-invalidate: a contradiction closes the old row’s validity and writes the replacement in the same operation, never an edit or a bare delete. The judge writes episodic facts freely but cannot mint durable rows; durable promotion still requires the confirmed-proposal path. Each category has its own half-life (events fade in days, preferences persist while used) instead of one episodic window.

The utterance log is what makes “what did nadia say to get at the store?” answerable even when nadia said it to bramble: sealed rows of speaker, text, time, and whether the robot was being addressed. The gates are hard and live at capture, not recall: only a confirmed, enrolled household voice is ever transcribed and logged; the media veto keeps TV dialogue out; per-person opt-out and soft-mute veto capture entirely. Ambient capture ships dark (config off) until the Pi duty-cycle number is measured and recorded. Recall is gated too: household members only, and a person can always hear and delete what was captured of their own speech.

Memory rows record the embedder that wrote them, and the vector pass skips rows written by a different embedder instead of scoring garbage, so an embedder swap can no longer break recall silently; a re-embed job migrates old rows while docked. Recall and truthfulness have eval sets with recorded baselines, so a regression in either is a failing number, not a vibe.

Who has the floor

Attention (brain/attention.py) is a reflex: it decides whether a cue earns a turn right now, with dwell, habituation and a rate cap. It has no idea a conversation is happening, so its few seconds of dwell were the only thing keeping a second voice from yanking the body away from the person the robot was mid-answer with. The engagement arbiter (brain/engagement.py) holds the missing state: the floor (who the robot is with, named when speaker-ID knows, otherwise the bearing), bids from everyone else (ambient, social, urgent, safety), and break points (the moment a reply finishes, when switching is cheap).

The rules are the ones a person uses. A voice interjecting while the robot is mid-reply is a bid: one glance, and the real response waits for the break point, where the sociable default answers with a short aside through the proactivity gate (a reserved persona lets the glance be the whole answer - that flag is the persona layer’s to own). Between turns, a second voice is simply the next partner: families address the robot in rotation, and refusing the rotation would be worse than any interruption. Distress (a scream, a cry, any safety-flagged sound) never queues: it interrupts mid-word, floor or no floor. “Stop” is exempt from all of it - care rule 4 belongs to everyone in the room.

The arbiter decides; it moves nothing. Decisions become attention cues (and one gated greeting), so care, the reflexes and the mode envelope still sit above every turn it causes. The eyes-only glance is currently a low-rank cue that attention’s own restraint suppresses mid-dwell; it upgrades to a real directed gaze when the face controller grows one.

Meeting people

The robot recognizes enrolled voices per utterance, but a voice can only ever make a tentative identity (a recording or an imitation must not unlock anyone’s private world), and until this work it had no social behavior around not knowing someone: it never said hello to a stranger, never introduced itself, never learned a name. The introductions design (plans/introductions-plan.md, with the field research that shaped it) adds that layer without bending the identity rules.

One new identity tier: claimed, below tentative. A claimed name is a name someone offered in conversation (“I’m Sam”, or the host saying “this is Sam”). It lives in the brain’s memory only, dies with the conversation, and is used for phrasing alone. It is never passed to the dialogue as the speaker, because the speaker keys the utterance log, recall, persona, and role; it rides a separate context field that only reply wording reads.

The behavior follows the conversation-analysis literature rather than instinct. Names are offered, never demanded: the robot’s one-time self-introduction ends on its own name and waits, because an offered name usually earns the other one back, and asking a stranger for personal information is a face-threatening act (and reads as surveillance coming from a machine). The host outranks the robot: when a household member is part of the conversation, the robot does not introduce itself at all. A hard annoyance budget is contract, not tuning: zero unprompted questions to strangers, one self-introduction per conversation, a name spoken at most twice per conversation, one plain apology when a name was wrong (the mistaken match is then treated as a stranger for the rest of the conversation, so nothing else gets logged under the wrong person).

The stranger signal itself is honest by construction: the speaker identifier answers “known”, “unknown”, or “unavailable”, and only “unknown” (a clear voice, far from everyone enrolled) means stranger. An empty gallery or a missing model is “unavailable”, so a fresh robot never treats its whole family as strangers. Two more refusals guard the failure every home assistant is complained about for, a child greeted as their parent: no identity claim on under two seconds of audio (verification error roughly doubles from four seconds of speech to two), and no claim on an ambiguous match, where the winner beats the runner-up by less than a margin - family voices genuinely cluster in embedding space, so a narrow win over a housemate is the misattribution case, not evidence. Matching scores each person’s centroid (the mean of their enrolled takes), the standard recipe, so an outlier take cannot buy a win. Actually remembering a person is a spoken ceremony with two consents. An adult opens the door (“remember Sam’s voice”, adult-gated and confirmed), and the person’s own yes is take zero: the robot says what it keeps in plain words and records nothing until they agree. Three short prompted takes follow, quality-gated (enough speech, a transcript the recognizer believed), then the sealed write, a role-only ledger line, and the payoff in the same breath: “Done. Hi, Sam!” A duplicate name is refused with a variant ask, never merged over an existing person. During a ceremony, utterances are takes, not turns: they never reach the dialogue or the utterance log. The web console lists enrolled voices (names and provenance, never the voiceprint itself) and can forget any of them; your own voice, like your own face, needs no capability to delete. A visitor who never enrolls leaves nothing behind: the claimed name lives in a RAM-only sketch that keeps two guests apart for the length of the visit and dies with the conversation.

The robot is whatever answered

The boot path used to be all-or-nothing: production booted only when the whole body answered, the simulator faked everything, and a head with a microphone on a bench was neither, so the only way to talk to the robot was a bench tool with in-process memory. The rule that replaced it: the robot is whatever hardware is attached, at the full capability of those parts, from the day those parts exist.

Every hardware-bundle member is optional. Discovery attaches whatever answers (the head’s devices today; the base drivers slot in when they land), the voice stack attaches independently, and a service whose device is absent simply does not start - health reports the capability as honestly missing, never faulted, the contract voice always had (“a robot that cannot hear is quiet, not broken”). Safety keeps its shape at every size: the care service runs regardless, and a machine where nothing at all answers still refuses to boot rather than arming a fake-healthy robot. Nothing is faked outside the simulator’s explicit flag.

The test that pins the rule is the product promise in miniature: boot the full app with only ears, tell it where the scissors are, boot a second app on the same data directory, and ask for the memory back.

Booting on power-up is the systemd unit (deploy/systemd/maipai-bot.service, one process hosting every service, restarted by systemd if it dies, each service restarted inside by the supervisor if it ticks wrong). And the screen lights when the robot does: the touchscreen renderer - UI-01’s long- pending pixel half - draws the composed PanelFrame to the DSI framebuffer. The layout is the owner’s, settled on the bench (2026-09-01) after two wrong drafts (a presence ring, then a face that duplicated the head’s): three always-on icon columns - battery, internet, mode - in universal forms only, no words (the design’s own non-negotiable, re-learned the hard way). Battery is the classic silhouette with a real fill from the UPS HAT (E) driver, or a wall plug on a body with no battery; internet is the wifi fan reading the default route (a purely local fact - tailscale’s self-view falsely called a connected bench offline); mode carries the status vocabulary with a green check for ready. Unknowns draw as grey plus the question symbol, never guessed. The recording dot and the one banner strip remain; setup shows the shared-title QR page. Redraws happen only on change, and a dead panel is reported once and never costs the conversation.

The robot speaks its own body

The whatever-answered rule gives every boot a different body, so the dialogue has to know which parts exist and answer accordingly. The research is consistent about how (Kwon, Huang and Dragan, HRI 2018; LeMasurier et al., HRI 2024; the trust-repair meta-analyses): refuse at ask time from the live capability model rather than attempting and failing, name the goal and the body-grounded cause rather than deflecting (“I can’t get to the kitchen - I don’t have wheels yet”, never “I can’t do that”), end forward-looking with a real plan or a real fix, and shorten on repetition because repeated apology decays to whining (“Still no wheels!”). Concrete part-facts are also how children build a correct model of the robot’s mind: no camera means it can’t see, and saying exactly that beats magic.

The implementation (cognition/body.py) rides the existing health registry: the capability view now carries the degraded set, so answers distinguish missing (“I don’t have a camera yet”) from warming (“my camera’s here, but not ready”) from working. Every motion skill gates on the body at ask time; a trip is also refused on a KNOWN low battery in task terms (“I’d run out partway”), never percent-speak, and an unreported gauge never blocks (absence of evidence is not an empty battery). “What do you see?” answers from the capability model and the awareness situation line, never the chat model, so a camera-less robot can never hallucinate a scene. Unplanned parts (arms) are answered honestly instead of promised.

The eye runs on picamera2, not a GStreamer pad-probe

The perception seam was designed as a GStreamer pad-probe before any hardware existed. The board that arrived is a Hailo-10H, and the hailotools GStreamer elements ship with the Hailo-8 TAPPAS stack; Raspberry Pi’s supported camera-plus-HAT path on the Pi 5 is picamera2’s Hailo helper over HailoRT, whose scheduler shares the device between the four HEFs. So the driver (hal/drivers/hailo_vision.py) captures frames with picamera2 and runs each model directly, which also yields the raw frame for free: dominant-color naming, snapshots for the text readers and the VLM, all without a second camera owner. The seam contract is unchanged - Detector for people, ObjectSpotter for things, track continuity and gallery matching above the HAL. Open items are in the hardware record: the H10 HEF availability (the vision pack is declared but unpinned), the NMS output layout the adapters assume, and rpicam-still contention while the pipeline owns the camera.

Recognition is household-scoped (decided 2026-09-01)

The robot names what it SEES generically (a truck, a dog, a red cup) and names WHO only for people this household enrolled, face and voice alike. Two capabilities are deliberately out, both technically easy on this hardware:

  • No license plate reading. Logging strangers’ plates is surveillance of people outside the household and legally fraught in several places. The wanted use cases (“a FedEx truck out front”, “someone pulled in”) are covered by scene fusion and the presence radar without ever reading a plate. If “whose car just arrived” for the household’s OWN cars is ever wanted, that is a separate, enrolled, per-car decision to record here first.
  • No celebrity/famous-person gallery. Shipping embeddings of real, identifiable, non-consenting people would flip enrolled-face personalization into ambient identification of strangers. “Who is that actor?” is a question for the read-this-to-me VLM mode, asked about a screen, not an always-on recognizer.

Both fit the standing product rule: identity personalizes, it never surveils, and it exists only for people who taught the robot who they are.

The hub owning a feature never keeps it off the robot (Jesse, 2026-09-02)

An early planning pass rejected on-robot media control, media identification and local target discovery because MaiPai Home already carries them. Jesse overruled the reasoning itself, not just the verdicts: the robot is standalone first, so duplicating a hub capability on-board is the point. “The hub does this” is never an argument in a design discussion here.

What survives of those rejections is placement-independent: the pace rules (every outbound feature behind the context layer’s single limiter, back off on the first signal) and the privacy rules. Both apply to a feature wherever it lives, and neither is about which machine runs the code.

BLE presence: registered devices only, and the filter sits at the edge

Jesse wants the robot to take nearby-hints from the family’s own devices and tags (a watch says “Dad is around”; a tracker on the keys feeds the finder), and to help the follow stage. The line that keeps this personalization rather than surveillance is the same one recognition already draws: identity exists only for people (and now things) the household enrolled.

  • Only registered addresses survive the driver. The bleak scan callback checks the live registry and drops every other advertisement on the floor: not buffered, not counted, not logged. The service drops unregistered adverts again (defense in depth), and the tests pin both edges.
  • Addresses never reach the bus. A hardware address is a stable identifier, so the presence heartbeat carries names, kinds and buckets only; the address lives in the sealed-permission registry file alone.
  • Proximity is coarse on purpose. RSSI is not a ruler: near / close / far / away, smoothed and hysteresis-guarded, is everything it can honestly say. No trilateration, no position log - presence drives what the robot does now, exactly like the radar rule.
  • Passive network sniffing stays out. Watching unregistered Wi-Fi or BLE devices is surveillance with extra steps; the wanted capability is fully served by opt-in registration.

Product lookup ships on, the weather rule (Jesse, 2026-09-02)

The context layer’s standing rule is that only weather and severe alerts ship on and every other outbound is off until enabled. Jesse put product lookup on the weather side of that line: naming a scanned product is a thing a family expects to just work, it fires only around a deliberate physical act (a code in front of the camera), and the payload is the barcode digits with no identity. So barcode.online_lookup defaults on, the privacy page says so in its table, the switch remains, and every hit is learned locally so lookups decay toward zero in a settled household. This is a recorded exception to the feeds-ship-off rule, not a repeal of it.
