Build the head, on the bench

By the end of this page the robot has a face: two eyes that blink and emote, a dot-matrix mouth that moves when it talks, a camera that looks around on a pan-tilt, and a privacy shutter it can report on. Everything sits loose on the bench. The body comes later, and nothing you wire here changes when it does.

What you need for this page

Parts (specs in the parts list)

  • The Pi, set up per Prepare the Pi
  • 1 face controller: Raspberry Pi Pico 2, and a USB cable for it
  • 2 eye displays: 2.0” 240x320 ST7789, SPI, 3.3 V
  • 2 mouth panels: WS2812B 8x8
  • 1 level shifter: 74AHCT125
  • Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 + a 22-to-15-pin Pi 5 camera cable, 50 cm
  • 1 lever microswitch with a 2-wire lead (the shutter sensor)
  • A 5 V, 3 A power supply with bare leads

Software

  • The robot software with its hardware add-ons, installed on the Pi in Prepare the Pi (uv sync --extra hardware)
  • i2cdetect and rpicam-still from that page’s apt line
  • scp or similar on your other computer, to look at the camera picture

Tools

  • The 40-pin ribbon cable, a T-type GPIO breakout, and a breadboard
  • Jumper wires, about 40
  • A small Phillips screwdriver
  • A soldering iron with a temperature setting, and a 2.4 mm chisel tip. The eye displays ship with their 8-pin header loose in the bag, so there is soldering on this page whether you like it or not. Set it to about 650 F (340 C). A wattage number on the box is not temperature control: look for a dial or a display
  • Solder: 63/37 tin-lead, rosin core, 0.6 mm. It sets at a single temperature instead of going through a soft stage, so a joint that moves slightly while it cools still comes out sound. Lead-free is legal, harder to work with, and not worth it for eight joints
  • Something that holds the board so both your hands are free: helping-hands clips or a small PCB clamp. Clamp the bare board edge, never the glass or the ribbon
  • A magnifying lamp. 2.54 mm pins are close together and a bridge between two of them is invisible at arm’s length
  • A multimeter (optional, but it ends most arguments with a wire)

One afternoon, two if the LED layout or eye orientation needs sorting out.

Ground rules for the bench

  • Power off while wiring. Pull the Pi’s USB-C and the 5 V supply before touching a wire. Both, every time.

  • Two supplies, one ground. The Pi runs from its own USB-C supply. The LED panels and the servos run from the 5 V bench supply. Connect the 5 V supply’s negative to a Pi GND pin so they share a ground. Never feed the LEDs or servos from the Pi’s 5 V pins: 128 LEDs at full white pull almost 8 A, and a servo’s start-up spike will reboot the Pi.

  • Wire to the breakout, not the Pi. The 40-pin ribbon (the flat cable with a plug on each end) connects the pins on top of the AI HAT to a GPIO breakout: a T-shaped “GPIO extension board” pushed into a breadboard is the usual one. Press one plug straight down onto the HAT’s pins with the cable’s edge wire (pin 1, brown on a rainbow cable, or the red stripe on a grey one) at the corner nearest the Pi’s SD card slot, away from the USB ports; the other plug is keyed and only fits the breakout one way. Every wire on this page then goes into the breadboard row next to the matching label, and the pins on the HAT are never touched again. Pin names below are BCM GPIO numbers, with the physical header pin in parentheses. (In the body stage the head wires are re-run through the tower to a screw-terminal breakout so nothing shakes loose while driving; that board comes with the body, not now.)

  • Test each part before adding the next. Every step ends with a command that proves the part works. If it doesn’t, stop and fix it; stacking a second unknown on top of the first is how afternoons disappear.

  1. The eyes

    Both displays share the SPI bus and the DC and reset lines. Only the chip select (CS) line differs: it is what makes one display the left eye and the other the right.

    Display pinLeft eyeRight eye
    GNDGND (pin 6)GND (pin 9)
    VCC3.3 V (pin 1)3.3 V (pin 17)
    SCLGPIO 11 (pin 23)GPIO 11 (pin 23)
    SDAGPIO 10 (pin 19)GPIO 10 (pin 19)
    RST (sometimes RES)GPIO 25 (pin 22)GPIO 25 (pin 22)
    DCGPIO 6 (pin 31)GPIO 6 (pin 31)
    CSGPIO 8 (pin 24)GPIO 7 (pin 26)
    BL (sometimes BLK)3.3 V (pin 1)3.3 V (pin 17)

    Reaching the GPIO past the AI HAT. The AI HAT+ 2 ships with a 16 mm stacking header that carries it above the Pi. That header is exactly long enough to reach the HAT and no further, so its pins fill the wells you can see on top and there is nothing left to connect to. Pressing pins in from above does not work, and a Dupont jumper cannot reach a contact.

    Swap that stock header for an extra-tall 2x20 stacking header. The Pi’s own 40-pin header is soldered to the board and is never touched; what gets replaced is the HAT’s 16 mm one:

    • Lift the AI HAT off and pull out the 16 mm header that came with it.
    • Fit the extra-tall header in its place: socket down onto the Pi’s soldered pins, long legs pointing up.
    • Put the HAT back on the legs. They now pass all the way through and stand proud on top, which is where the ribbon connects.
    • The HAT rides a few millimetres higher, so build the standoffs up from the M2.5 kit to support all four corners, and check the PCIe ribbon still reaches.

    You’ll know it worked when the Hailo still enumerates: lspci | grep -i hailo lists it, and robot.bench.head check says ai-hat: ok.

    Ribbon, breakout, breadboard. Press the 40-pin ribbon onto those exposed pins, plug its other end into the T-shaped GPIO extension board (that end is keyed), and push the T board into the breadboard so it straddles the centre groove, one row of its pins each side, pressed fully flat.

    Check the ribbon is not reversed, and measure rather than look. The HAT end is not keyed, so a 180 degree rotation silently swaps every signal. With the Pi powered and nothing else connected, put the meter on DC volts, black probe on the metal USB shell, red probe in the T board’s 3V3 row. 3.3 V means correct; 0 V means flip the connector. Nothing can be harmed at this point because the display is not attached yet, which is exactly why the check comes first.

    The breadboard holes. Rows a b c d e are one connected node and f g h i j are another; the groove separates them. So a wire in row a reaches the T board pin sitting in row c of the same column. The power rails along the edges are not used here.

    Note that many breakout boards print SPI names rather than GPIO numbers for three of these pins:

    Display pinT board labelSame as
    VCC3V3pin 1
    GNDGNDpin 9
    BLthe second 3V3pin 17
    SDASPIMOSIGPIO 10
    SCLSPISCLKGPIO 11
    RSTGPIO25GPIO 25
    DCGPIO6GPIO 6

    With the T board’s bottom label row seated in c and its top row in g, both starting at column 1, the left eye’s eight wires land in: 1a (VCC), 5a (GND), 9a (BL), 10a (SDA), 12a (SCL), 16a (DC), 11j (RST), 12j (CS). Meter 1a for 3.3 V before connecting the display, to confirm the column count matches your board.

    The 8-pin header usually ships loose in the bag. Solder it with the pins out the back, away from the screen. For a first bench test you can wedge it in unsoldered, but if the picture flickers or comes and goes, solder it before changing any driver setting: a marginal contact looks exactly like a wrong init sequence.

    Stand the displays portrait (tall), side by side, about 5.5 inches apart centre to centre. That is where they sit in the body, with the camera between them.

    Power up and run:

    You’ll know it worked when both eyes light up amber on black and cycle through the expressions shown above, blinking now and then, then settle on neutral.

    If the picture is sideways or upside down, rerun with --rotate 90 (or 180, 270) until it is upright. If the wink closes the wrong eye, add --swap. If the pupils drift the wrong way, add --mirror. The command prints the flags you settled on; keep them, you’ll need them in the next stage’s settings.

  2. The mouth

    The two 8x8 panels chain into one 16-wide by 8-tall matrix. Data goes through the level shifter, because the LEDs want a 5 V signal and a 3.3 V one “sort of works” and then flickers for the rest of the robot’s life. Don’t skip the chip.

    The mouth is not driven by the Pi

    A Pi 5 cannot drive WS2812B pixels. The usual library, rpi_ws281x, works by taking over the BCM283x PWM and DMA blocks, and the Pi 5 has neither: its GPIO lives on the separate RP1. It fails with ws2811_init failed with code -3 on every pin, and no wiring change fixes it.

    The mouth runs from the face controller instead, a Pico 2 that also drives the eyes. Wire the panels as below and test them at that stage. The driver refuses on a Pi 5 rather than crashing, so if you run the command here you get one line telling you this.

    The level shifter. It is a DIP-14: push it into the breadboard straddling the centre groove, notch pointing left, which puts pin 1 at the bottom left and pin 14 directly above it.

    WireFromTo
    redbench supply +chip pin 14 (VCC)
    blackbench supply −chip pin 7 (GND)
    blackchip pin 7 (GND)chip pin 1 (1OE)
    whiteany Pi GND pinchip pin 7 (GND)
    greenPi GPIO 19 (pin 35)chip pin 2 (1A)
    greenchip pin 3 (1Y)panel 1 DIN

    Pin 1 is the enable and it is active low: grounded means the buffer passes signal, left floating means the chip outputs nothing at all and the mouth stays dark with no other symptom. The white wire is the one that catches people out: without a shared ground between the Pi and the LED supply, the data line has no reference and the panels ignore it.

    The panels.

    WireFromTo
    redbench supply +panel 1 5V
    whitebench supply −panel 1 GND
    greenpanel 1 DOUTpanel 2 DIN
    redbench supply +panel 2 5V
    whitebench supply −panel 2 GND

    Panel power comes straight from the supply, not through the breadboard: 128 LEDs pull more current than breadboard rails are happy carrying.

    Read the silkscreen, do not trust the lead colours. Each panel has its pads labelled 5V, GND, DIN and DOUT, and an arrow showing which way data flows. On common panels the flying lead is red, white and green, and green is the data wire while white is ground, which is not what the colours suggest. Swapping those two puts 5 V into the data line.

    Lay the panels side by side with panel 1 on the robot’s right (your left, facing it), coplanar, just below the eyes.

    Run the layout pattern first. It lights one LED at a time and prints its index, so you can see how the panels are wired inside (row by row, or snaking back and forth) and whether the second panel is turned around:

    Watch pixel 0 through 15: if they run left to right on one row, then pixel 16 starts the next row on the same side, the layout is plain rows. If pixel 8 comes back the other way, it’s a snake: add --serpentine. If the second panel runs backwards or upside down, add --rotate-second. Then:

    You’ll know it worked when a dim amber line sits in the centre, and the “talk” pattern pulses outward from it like a voice would, with no flicker and nothing lit at the corners.

    The brightness is capped in software at 25 percent and the cap cannot be raised past 40. That limit is what keeps the mouth inside the robot’s power budget; it is not a suggestion.

    If the pattern is right but the LEDs never light, the Pi’s PIO output on GPIO 19 may need attention: this is the one part of the head design still being verified on hardware. See Fix a problem.

  3. The camera

    Plug the 22-to-15-pin cable into the Pi’s CAM/DISP 1 connector (the one nearer the board edge; CAM/DISP 0 beside it already has the touchscreen) and into the Camera Module 3. Lift each connector’s latch fully before inserting; the cable seats only one way, metal contacts facing the same direction as the screen cable’s in the connector beside it.

    The official Camera Module 3 needs no configuration. The Arducam version needs two config lines first; they’re in Fix a problem.

  4. The pan-tilt (superseded)

    Caution

    This step describes the old fixed-head design, where a small pan-tilt aimed the camera behind the visor. The head now moves as a whole and aims the camera itself, so this step is being replaced by the neck build. Skip it.

    Assemble the Arducam pan-tilt platform following Arducam’s own guide, and mount the Camera Module 3 on it with the camera cable looped so it can nod without pulling. Centre both servos by hand before you screw the arms on: the software assumes “arm straight” is the middle of travel.

    The kit’s servo board is a PCA9685 on I2C. Wire it:

    GNDGND (pin 6)
    V+ (servo power)5 V from the bench supply

  5. The privacy shutter switch

    In the body, an opaque slide covers the camera and a microswitch reports whether the slide is closed. On the bench, wire just the switch:

    Switch terminalConnect to
    COMGND (pin 14)
    NOGPIO 16 (pin 36)

    You’ll know it worked when pressing the lever prints CLOSED and releasing prints OPEN. The robot reports this state on its screen and knob later; the slide itself is what actually blinds the camera, the switch only tells the truth about it.

  6. The demo

    Everything at once:

    Add the eye and mouth flags you settled on above.

    The eyes open from closed, the head looks around and the eyes follow, the mouth pulses through a pretend sentence, it blinks, and it gets sleepy. Take a video. Keep the flags the eyes needed, the panel layout, and the servo range: the next stage’s settings use them.

What’s not on the bench yet

The touch electrodes (copper tape inside the shell, read by an MPR121), the smoked visor, and the shutter slide itself all need the body. The ears and the real voice are their own stage. Until then the bench USB microphone and speaker stand in for them.
