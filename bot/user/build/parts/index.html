Parts list

Parts are listed by the stage that needs them. Buy a stage when you start it, not all at once: return windows expire, and later stages are still being designed.

Where a spec matters, it is in bold. Search phrases work on any big retailer; we do not link listings because they change weekly.

What the whole robot costs

Planning figures, not quotes. They are what the design budgeted per stage as of 2026-08-19, repriced at checkout every time — parts move, and some listings named in the design have already gone out of stock.

Two things that figure does not include: the braked drivetrain, which is the one part still being chosen and carries a $150–350 allowance, and tools, which are listed per page rather than bought as a kit.

You do not spend this at once. Each stage stands alone, and the first two — core and the head — are a working, talking robot head on a bench for about $940 before anything moves.

The head is being redesigned

The head is becoming a moving head: it pans and tilts on a neck instead of sitting fixed on the body. That change is decided but the build pages have not been rewritten around it yet. Two consequences are already reflected below: the camera pan-tilt is gone, because the head aims the camera now, and the neck servos take its place. Do not buy anything marked superseded.

Stage 1: the head

PartSpec that mattersNotes
Eye displays, 22.0 inch, 240x320, ST7789, SPI, 3.3 V logic and power, 8-pin header (GND, VCC, SCL, SDA, RES, DC, CS, BLK)Sold as 2-packs. Confirm the listing says ST7789 and 3.3 V.
Mouth LED panels, 2WS2812B 8x8, 64 pixels each, with DIN/DOUT padsSome listings say “256” by mistake. Count the LEDs in the photo: 8 by 8. Do not buy an 8x32 strip.
Face controllerRaspberry Pi Pico 2 (RP2350), and a USB cable to reach the Pi. The Pico 2 W works too and is what this build uses; the wireless pins are clear of every pin the face needsDrives both eyes and the mouth. One board does all three: an eye on each of its two SPI blocks so they push at the same time, and the mouth on PIO. The Pi cannot do any of it, which is the reason this part exists.
Level shifter74AHCT125, DIP-14Turns the face controller’s 3.3 V data signal into the 5 V the LEDs want. Still needed with the Pico, which is a 3.3 V part like the Pi. One chip, usually sold in 10-packs.
CameraRaspberry Pi Camera Module 3 (standard, 75 degrees)The wide version loses close-up detail. The Arducam compatible version works too, with two extra config lines (see Fix a problem).
Camera cablePi 5 camera cable, 22-pin to 15-pin, 50 cmThe short cable in the camera box does not reach once the camera is in the moving head. Get the long one now.
Camera pan-tiltSupersededThe moving head aims the camera, so a separate camera pan-tilt is no longer bought.
Neck pan-tilt2-DOF servo pan-tilt kit, metal servos, without control board (Yahboom class: a 20 kg 270 degree pan servo, a 25 kg 180 degree tilt servo, aluminium bracket with bearings)This is the neck. Ordered 2026-08-31. Verify the mounting plate takes an adapter for a head about 10 inches across, and that both servos are in the box.
Servo driverPCA9685 16-channel 12-bit I2C PWM board (Dorhea class)Drives the two neck servos on channels 0 and 1. VCC from the Pi’s 3.3 V, V+ from the servo supply. Clear the ALLCALL bit at init: the chip also answers on 0x70, which is the ToF multiplexer’s address. Ordered 2026-08-31.
Joint encoders, 2AS5600 12-bit magnetic rotary encoder on I2C, each with its diametrically-magnetised magnetHow the robot knows where its head actually is. A hobby servo is told an angle and never reports back, so a stall, a slipped horn and a clean move look identical. These read the joint itself. All AS5600 share address 0x36 and cannot be re-strapped, so the two go on separate multiplexer channels. Ordered 2026-08-31.
Motion sensorMPU-6050 6-axis accelerometer and gyroscope on I2C (GY-521 module)How the robot knows it has been picked up, moved, or tipped over. Bought 2026-09-01. It cannot tell which way the robot is facing; that comes from the lidar later.
Distance sensorsVL53L5CX multi-zone time-of-flight, wide fieldHow the robot sees obstacles. Each one returns an 8x8 grid of distances rather than a single reading, so it can tell a doorway from a wall. They all share one address, which is why the design puts them behind an I2C multiplexer. Bought 2026-09-01.
Presence sensors2x 24 GHz mmWave human micro-motion radar, FMCW, with UART and GPIO outputHow the robot knows someone is in the room. Unlike a PIR it still sees a person who is sitting perfectly still, because it picks up their breathing, and it works in the dark. Bought 2026-09-01.
I2C multiplexerTCA9548A 8-channel I2C multiplexerEvery distance sensor answers on the same address and so does every joint encoder, so they cannot share a bus. This gives each one its own channel. Strap A0 high to move it to 0x71: at its default 0x70 it collides with the servo driver’s all-call address, and the symptom is distance readings that corrupt whenever a servo moves. Ordered 2026-09-01.
Ambient light sensorVEML7700 on I2CHow the robot knows the room is dark, so every lit surface dims together. Without it the robot falls back to the clock, which is wrong for a nap behind drawn shades and wrong for a bright kitchen at midnight.
Microphone4-mic USB array with echo cancelling and direction finding (XVF3800 class)The robot’s ears. Plugs into a USB 2 port directly, never behind a hub: it streams isochronous audio and a hub causes dropouts. The header pins in its bag are for expansion pads this build does not use.
SpeakerAny small powered speaker with a 3.5 mm input, plus a USB charger for itPlugs into the mic array’s line-out, not the Pi. The Pi 5 has no headphone jack, and echo cancelling only works if the robot’s own voice passes through the array. Power it from a wall charger so its amplifier does not put hum on the Pi’s rail.
5 V supply5 V, 3 A or more, bare leads or barrel adapterFor the mouth LEDs. Never power them from the Pi’s pins. The neck servos want their own bigger supply, below.

Later stages (decided so far)

These are the parts already settled in the design. Prices and exact listings get filled in when each stage is written up.

StageKey parts
The baseA screw-terminal GPIO breakout (replaces the breadboard), a donor hoverboard (the wheels, motors, and 36 V battery), a plywood deck, two 3 to 4 inch rubber swivel casters, a 30 A breaker rated to interrupt DC at 42 V (an automotive breaker is not), a 22 mm emergency-stop button, bumper microswitches, a 36-to-12 V converter and fused distribution block, time-of-flight distance sensors, an ADuM1201 digital isolator for the serial link to the motor board, and an ST-Link V2 to flash that board’s firmware once. The drivetrain’s spring brakes are still being chosen.
The body and faceA three-tier plastic nightstand (about 12.6 by 12.6 by 22.6 inches) as the shell, smoked acrylic for the visor, a second Pico 2 as the body controller (the underglow’s timing cannot cross the neck’s rotating joint), a round-screen rotary “heart” knob, a hidden speaker pair, a 60 mm fan, a capacitive touch controller with copper tape.
Follow and carryA fold-flat wire basket with load cells, an ultra-wideband tag pair.
Navigate and dockA 360-degree lidar (RPLIDAR C1, bought 2026-09-01, parked until the body exists), a rear camera, a charging dock with spring contacts and a certified mains switch.
