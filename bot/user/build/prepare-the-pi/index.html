Prepare the Pi

Only the robot-specific setup is here. For putting an operating system on a Pi in general, follow Raspberry Pi’s own getting started guide.

What you need for this page

Parts

  • Raspberry Pi 5 (16 GB) with the Active Cooler fitted
  • Official 27 W USB-C power supply
  • The M.2 SSD in its USB 3 enclosure (the boot drive)
  • The 5 inch DSI touchscreen (Freenove, 800x480) with the 50 cm Pi 5 DSI cable, 22-pin to 15-pin, from the parts list. Use that one, not the short cable in the screen’s box: the long one is what reaches the screen inside the body, and what goes on now stays on.
  • 2 female-to-female jumper wires (screen power)
  • No microSD card: the robot boots from the SSD

Software (on your other computer)

  • Raspberry Pi Imager
  • A terminal with ssh (built into macOS, Linux, and Windows 11)

Tools

  • Another computer on the same Wi-Fi as the Pi, with a free USB port for the enclosure while you flash it

Not needed: a keyboard, mouse, or HDMI monitor. The robot’s own touchscreen is the Pi’s screen from the first boot, so you can watch it start up, but you type nothing on the Pi: steps 1 and 2 happen on your other computer, and from step 5 on you talk to the Pi over Wi-Fi in an ssh session from that same computer.

No wiring on this page. About 30 minutes, most of it waiting on downloads.

  1. Put the SSD in its enclosure and plug the enclosure into your other computer.

  2. Flash Raspberry Pi OS Lite (64-bit) onto the SSD, from your other computer. Open Raspberry Pi Imager there, choose Raspberry Pi 5, then Raspberry Pi OS Lite (64-bit), then the enclosure as the storage device. Lite, not Desktop: the robot has no monitor and the desktop only costs memory. In the imager’s settings:

    • Hostname: maipai-bot
    • Username: maipai (this exact name; the robot’s software runs as this account later). Password: your choice, and write it down.
    • Wi-Fi: your network name and password
    • Services: turn on SSH and pick Use password authentication. (The other choice, public-key only, is fine if you already use SSH keys and paste your public key in; if you’re not sure, that’s not you.)
    • Raspberry Pi Connect: leave it off. It’s a remote-access service that goes through Raspberry Pi’s cloud, and nothing on this robot goes through anyone’s cloud. You reach the Pi over your own Wi-Fi.

    The Pi is still in its box for this step.

  3. Connect the touchscreen. With the Pi unpowered, plug the 50 cm 22-to-15-pin DSI cable into the Pi’s CAM/DISP 0 connector (the one farther from the board edge; CAM/DISP 1 next to it is the camera’s, later) and into the screen’s DSI connector. Lift each latch fully; the cable seats only one way. The screen takes its power from two header pins:

    ScreenPi header
    5Vpin 4 (5 V)
    GNDpin 34 (GND)

    Pi OS detects this screen on its own, no settings. On a Lite system it shows the text console: boot messages, then a login prompt. You will not type at it (no keyboard), but you can see what the Pi is doing.

  4. Move the enclosure to the Pi and boot. Plug it into the bottom blue USB 3 port (the blue pair is USB 3; the black pair is USB 2 and is spoken for by other parts later). The bottom blue port is the boot drive’s port for good; the top blue one is kept free for a hub in the body stage. A Pi 5 boots from USB out of the box when no SD card is present. Power up.

    How you know it’s up and reachable, in order:

    1. The screen scrolls boot messages, then settles on a maipai-bot login: prompt with a line above it reading My IP address is .... That is the Pi on your Wi-Fi. Takes a minute or two on the first boot (it grows the file system and reboots once).
    2. From your other computer, ping maipai-bot.local answers (press Ctrl-C to stop it). If the name doesn’t resolve, use the IP address from the screen instead, in this and every later command.
    3. ssh maipai@maipai-bot.local asks for the password you set and gives you a maipai@maipai-bot:~ $ prompt.

    Stay in that session: everything from here on is typed into it, and that is how the robot is managed for good. The touchscreen only shows you what the Pi is doing.

    If it never appears on the network, the Pi’s bootloader may be old: use Imager’s Misc Utility Images → Bootloader → USB Boot on any spare SD card, boot the Pi from that once (green LED blinks steadily when done), remove it, and try the SSD again.
